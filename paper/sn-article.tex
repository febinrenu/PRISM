\title[Do extracted statutory rules reach the expert's conclusions?]{Same Statute, Same Conclusions? Measuring How Errors in Language-Model Rule Extraction Propagate to Tax-Policy Conclusions}

\author*[1]{\fnm{Febin K} \sur{Renu}}\email{[corresponding author e-mail]}

\affil*[1]{\orgname{[Affiliation]}, \orgaddress{\country{India}}}

\abstract{% RESULTS: the abstract's findings sentence is written after the final runs.
Large language models can read statutes and return rules in a structured form,
and a growing body of work proposes to turn such output into executable
"rules as code". Extraction quality is usually reported as precision and recall
of the extracted text. For policy analysis the question that matters is
different: when the extracted rules are executed on a population, do they
lead to the same policy conclusions as rules coded by an expert, and if not,
which extraction errors are responsible? We study this question for Indian
personal income tax. A structure-aware parser turns five Finance Acts and the
Income-tax Act 2025 into abstract syntax trees with exact offsets; language
models and a rule-based baseline extract grounded rules with typed,
executable effects; one deterministic assembler turns every system's rules
into tax parameters; and a static microsimulation calibrated to the
published Income Tax Return Statistics evaluates pre-registered conclusions
about revenue, progressivity and the distribution of gains. Divergences are
attributed to individual provisions with exact Shapley values, tested for
robustness against population uncertainty, and set against a controlled
study that injects typed extraction errors into the expert law.}

\keywords{Rules as code, Statutory interpretation, Large language models,
Information extraction, Tax microsimulation, Explainability, Indian tax law}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Legislative drafting offices, tax administrations and researchers increasingly
want statutes in a form a computer can execute. Hand-coding a statute is slow
and requires legal and programming expertise at once, which is why large
language models (LLMs) that read a provision and return its rules in a
structured form are attractive. Their output is usually evaluated as an
information-extraction task: are the right spans, modalities and numbers
recovered? That evaluation answers a question about text. A policy analyst
asks a question about consequences: if this machine-read version of the law
is used to estimate the revenue cost of a reform or its effect on
progressivity, will the analyst reach the same conclusion as with an expert's
coding of the same law?

The two questions can have different answers. A single misread number in a
rate table can change the tax of millions of people, while a mistake in a
procedural clause may change nothing that is measured. Surface metrics weight
both equally. This paper measures the second question directly.

\paragraph{Research questions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item[RQ1] Do rules extracted by language models from Indian income-tax
  statutes lead to the same pre-registered policy conclusions as rules coded
  by an expert, when both are executed on the same taxpayer population?
  \item[RQ2] When conclusions differ, which provisions, and which kinds of
  extraction error, are responsible?
  \item[RQ3] Are the differences larger than the uncertainty of the
  simulation itself?
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item An end-to-end, reproducible pipeline from statute PDF to policy
  conclusion, in which every simulated quantity is traceable to the statute
  span it was read from: a structure-aware statute parser, grounded rule
  extraction with typed executable effects, and one deterministic assembler
  shared by all extraction systems (\cref{sec:pipeline}).
  \item A propagation measure: pre-registered policy conclusions computed for
  expert-coded and extracted law on the same population, with exact Shapley
  attribution of every divergence to the provision that caused it
  (\cref{sec:measures}).
  \item An external validation of the microsimulation against published tax
  statistics and Budget figures, reported including its failures
  (\cref{sec:validation}).
  \item A controlled error-injection study that maps which kinds of extraction
  error change which conclusions (\cref{sec:injection}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{From statute to parameters}\label{sec:pipeline}

\subsection{Statute structure}
Indian statutes are typeset with running heads, margin line numbers,
marginal notes and footnotes, and Finance Acts insert quoted text into the
Acts they amend. The parser works on the PDF's text lines with their
positions. It removes running heads (lines repeated on adjacent pages),
margin numbers and footnotes, uses the table of contents as an oracle of the
expected sections, and builds a tree of Part, Chapter, Section,
sub-section, clause, sub-clause, proviso, Explanation, Schedule, Paragraph
and table row. Clause letters and roman numerals that share a form ("(i)")
are told apart by their siblings. Quoted insertions in Finance Acts are
parsed as nested provisions. Every node has exact character offsets into a
canonical text and a content-addressed identifier
(the first 16 hex digits of SHA-256 over statute, path and text), so a node's
identity changes exactly when its text or position does.

\subsection{Rules with typed effects}
A rule is one deontic statement (obligation, prohibition, permission, power,
deeming or definition) with its subject, conditions, action, consequence,
exceptions and cross-references. Each of these is a \emph{span}: offsets into
the canonical text of the provision it was read from. A rule also carries
typed \emph{effects}, the executable content: slab rows (lower and upper bound,
rate, regime, age band, assessment year), rebates, standard deductions,
surcharge bands and caps, cess, and, for other statutes, penalties, fees,
interest and time limits.

\subsection{Extraction systems}
All language models receive the same versioned prompt, one provision per
call, with the enclosing section's opening words as context when a long
provision is split. The systems are GPT-OSS-120B and GPT-OSS-20B and
Qwen3.8-27B (served by Groq), Gemini 3.8 Flash, the local models Phi-3.5-mini
and Gemma-2-2B (4-bit, through Ollama), and a rule-based baseline that reads
slab rows and fee clauses with patterns. Decoding is greedy (temperature 0,
fixed seed). GPT-OSS-20B runs at low reasoning effort: at the default effort
it spent its whole 4,096-token output budget on reasoning for two of the
experiment's provisions and returned no answer, and the free tier's limit of
8,000 tokens per minute rules out a larger budget.

Every quoted span must be found in the provision: exactly, after
normalising whitespace and quotation marks, or by fuzzy alignment with a
similarity of at least 90, in which case the span snaps to the statute's own
text. A field that cannot be grounded is rejected, and every number in an
effect is checked against its quoted span. Each extraction is repeated
$k=4$ times at temperature 0.7; the share of samples that reproduce a rule or
effect is reported as its self-consistency \citep{wang2023selfconsistency}.
All model outputs are cached under a key that covers the provider, model,
generation options, prompt and prompt version, temperature and seed, so a
rerun never calls a model twice and the paper's tables can be regenerated
without network access.

\subsection{One assembler for every system}
The experimental design names, for each reform, which provision determines
which parameter (\cref{tab:design}). The assembler starts from the expert
parameters of the same year and replaces only those targets, using only
effects of the right kind read from the named provision. Slab rows scoped to
the assessment year take precedence over unscoped rows, and only effects
stated for the target's regime (or for both regimes) are used. For a scalar
parameter, effects stated for the target's regime take precedence over those
stated for both; if the remaining candidates disagree the target is
\emph{ambiguous}. A schedule is accepted only if it starts at zero, has no
gap or overlap, ends with an open top band and has non-decreasing rates. Any
target that cannot be filled becomes a review item and the system's run has
\emph{no conclusion}: nothing is filled by default, and a system is never
credited with the expert's value.

\section{Taxpayer population and microsimulation}\label{sec:population}

No unit-level tax or survey data were available to us. The population is
therefore reconstructed from the published Income Tax Return Statistics
(individuals): the number of returns and total gross total income (GTI) in
each income range (table 2.1) are reproduced exactly by weighted points
placed within each range by a maximum-entropy density tilted to the range's
mean, with a Pareto tail in the open top range. Chapter VI-A deductions are
assigned by rank matching the GTI distribution to the returned-income
distribution (table 2.10). The salaried share of each range is the number of
returns reporting salary income in the same range (table 2.2) divided by the
range's returns. Reported GTI is net of the standard deduction of the data
year, which is added back for salaried taxpayers so that each simulated law
applies its own deduction. Populations for later years are aged with nominal
GDP.

Each simulated law is applied to every taxpayer by a calculator that follows
the statutory order: standard deduction, Chapter VI-A deductions (old regime
only), rounding of total income (s.288A), slab tax by age band, rebate with
marginal relief, surcharge with marginal relief and the new regime's cap,
cess, and rounding of tax (s.288B). When both regimes are available a
taxpayer files under the cheaper one. Outcomes are revenue, decile effective
tax rates, and the Kakwani \citep{kakwani1977measurement},
Reynolds--Smolensky and Suits indices.

\section{Measures}\label{sec:measures}

\subsection{Pre-registered conclusions}
For each reform the same population is taxed under the previous year's
expert law and under the reform-year law, either expert-coded or assembled
from a system's rules. Seventeen conclusions were fixed before any system
output was scored: the direction of the revenue change; whether the revenue
change is within 10\% of the expert's; the direction of the change in the
Kakwani index; the decile with the largest fall in effective rate; the
direction of the effective-rate change in each decile; and the regime chosen
by the taxpayer at the 50th, 90th and 99th percentile. For the earliest coded
year the comparison is with a proportional tax raising the same revenue.

Directions are read with materiality thresholds (revenue \rupee500 crore,
effective rate 0.01 percentage points, Kakwani 0.001), and the top gaining
decile is named only when some decile gains by more than the rate threshold.
These thresholds amend the pre-registered definitions: the controlled
error-injection study (\cref{sec:injection}), which uses no system output,
showed that the original near-exact sign tests let a one-rupee shift of a
band boundary flip direction conclusions on reforms with no true change.
Both versions are computed for every run and both are reported.

A system's \emph{flip rate} is the share of conclusions that differ from the
expert's; a run with no conclusion counts every conclusion as flipped.

\subsection{Attribution}
Let $T$ be the set of a reform's targets whose assembled value differs from
the expert's. For a coalition $S \subseteq T$, the hybrid law takes the
system's value for the targets in $S$ and the expert's for the rest, and
$v(S)$ is its divergence from the expert law: the revenue error, the $L_1$
distance between decile effective rates, or the number of flipped
conclusions. The Shapley value \citep{shapley1953value} of each target is
computed exactly over all $2^{|T|}$ coalitions, so the values sum to the
divergence of the full system law. The \emph{minimal restoring set} is the
smallest set of targets whose correction removes every flip.

\subsection{Robustness to population uncertainty}
The population rests on assumptions that the published statistics do not
pin down: the share of taxpayers who choose the cheaper regime (0.25--1),
the scale of Chapter VI-A deductions (0.5--1.5), the Pareto shape of the top
range (1.5--3), the share of taxpayers aged 60--80 (0--0.2) and income growth
beyond the published series (7--12\%). For each run the conclusions of the
expert law and of the system's law are recomputed on 64 scrambled Sobol
draws of these assumptions. A flip's \emph{persistence} is the share of
draws in which it remains; the \emph{noise floor} is the share of draws in
which the expert's own conclusion differs from its conclusion on the
calibrated population. Systems are compared on paired conclusions with
exact McNemar tests \citep{mcnemar1947note}, Holm-corrected
\citep{holm1979simple}.

\subsection{Which kinds of error matter?}\label{sec:injection}
Thirteen typed errors were injected into the expert law, one target at a time:
a dropped top or middle band, a band boundary moved by one lakh or by one
rupee, a bound read in crore instead of lakh, a rate read from the adjacent
row, the previous year's table, a dropped marginal-relief clause, a dropped
rebate, the previous year's rebate, a misread rebate amount, the rebate of
the other regime, and the previous year's standard deduction.
