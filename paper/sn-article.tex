\title[Do extracted statutory rules reach the expert's conclusions?]{Same Statute, Same Conclusions? Measuring How Errors in Language-Model Rule Extraction Propagate to Tax-Policy Conclusions}

\author*[1]{\fnm{Febin K} \sur{Renu}}\email{[corresponding author e-mail]}

\affil*[1]{\orgname{[Affiliation]}, \orgaddress{\country{India}}}

\abstract{%
Large language models can read statutes and return rules in a structured form,
and a growing body of work proposes to turn such output into executable
"rules as code". Extraction quality is usually reported as precision and recall
of the extracted text. For policy analysis the question that matters is
different: when the extracted rules are executed on a population, do they
lead to the same policy conclusions as rules coded by an expert, and if not,
which extraction errors are responsible? We study this question for Indian
personal income tax. A structure-aware parser turns five Finance Acts and the
Income-tax Act 2025 into abstract syntax trees with exact offsets; language
models and a rule-based baseline extract grounded rules with typed,
executable effects; one deterministic assembler turns every system's rules
into tax parameters; and a static microsimulation calibrated to the
published Income Tax Return Statistics evaluates pre-registered conclusions
about revenue, progressivity and the distribution of gains. Divergences are
attributed to individual provisions with exact Shapley values, tested for
robustness against population uncertainty, and set against a controlled
study that injects typed extraction errors into the expert law. Whenever
the rules of the strongest systems could be assembled into a complete law,
for up to \res{assembled@qwen3.8-27b} of \res{reforms} reforms, they reached
every one of the expert's conclusions. Their failures concentrated on the top
of rate schedules, on the regime a provision applies to and on tables whose
layout scatters their cells. The injection study shows that these errors
would have changed conclusions had structural validation of the executable
law not turned them into visible gaps.}

\keywords{Rules as code, Statutory interpretation, Large language models,
Information extraction, Tax microsimulation, Explainability, Indian tax law}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Legislative drafting offices, tax administrations and researchers increasingly
want statutes in a form a computer can execute. Hand-coding a statute is slow
and requires legal and programming expertise at once, which is why large
language models (LLMs) that read a provision and return its rules in a
structured form are attractive. Their output is usually evaluated as an
information-extraction task: are the right spans, modalities and numbers
recovered? That evaluation answers a question about text. A policy analyst
asks a question about consequences: if this machine-read version of the law
is used to estimate the revenue cost of a reform or its effect on
progressivity, will the analyst reach the same conclusion as with an expert's
coding of the same law?

The two questions can have different answers. A single misread number in a
rate table can change the tax of millions of people, while a mistake in a
procedural clause may change nothing that is measured. Surface metrics weight
both equally. This paper measures the second question directly.

\paragraph{Research questions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item[RQ1] Do rules extracted by language models from Indian income-tax
  statutes lead to the same pre-registered policy conclusions as rules coded
  by an expert, when both are executed on the same taxpayer population?
  \item[RQ2] When conclusions differ, which provisions, and which kinds of
  extraction error, are responsible?
  \item[RQ3] Are the differences larger than the uncertainty of the
  simulation itself?
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item An end-to-end, reproducible pipeline from statute PDF to policy
  conclusion, in which every simulated quantity is traceable to the statute
  span it was read from: a structure-aware statute parser, grounded rule
  extraction with typed executable effects, and one deterministic assembler
  shared by all extraction systems (\cref{sec:pipeline}).
  \item A propagation measure: pre-registered policy conclusions computed for
  expert-coded and extracted law on the same population, with exact Shapley
  attribution of every divergence to the provision that caused it
  (\cref{sec:measures}).
  \item An external validation of the microsimulation against published tax
  statistics and Budget figures, reported including its failures
  (\cref{sec:validation}).
  \item A controlled error-injection study that maps which kinds of extraction
  error change which conclusions (\cref{sec:injection}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}
% Every work cited here has had its bibliographic record checked (sn-article.bib).

\paragraph{Law as executable rules.}
Representing legislation as executable logic is as old as the field:
TAXMAN modelled corporate tax concepts \citep{mccarty1977taxman}, and the
British Nationality Act was written as a logic program
\citep{sergot1986british}. Later work formalised the defeasible and deontic
character of norms \citep{governatori2005representing} and the logical
structure of tax statutes \citep{lawsky2017logic}. Catala is a programming
language whose default logic follows the structure of statutory exceptions
\citep{merigoux2021catala}. The "rules as code" movement argues for
publishing such machine-consumable versions of rules alongside the legal
text \citep{mohun2020cracking}. Tax-benefit microsimulation models
implement legislation to estimate its effects on populations: EUROMOD for
the European Union \citep{sutherland2013euromod}, and the open-source
OpenFisca \citep{openfisca} and PolicyEngine \citep{ahmadi2026policyengine}
engines. In all of these the rules are coded by people.

\paragraph{Language models and statutes.}
SARA introduced statutory reasoning over sections of the US Internal Revenue
Code with hand-written Prolog \citep{holzenberger2020dataset}, and
GPT-3 was found to make errors in reasoning over those statutes
\citep{blairstanek2023gpt3}. Tax-law question answering by successive LLMs
was studied by \citet{nay2024tax}. Recent work uses LLMs to produce
executable or formal versions of tax law: a benchmark for translating French
tax law into Catala \citep{lorenzo2025translating}, translation of statutes
and case facts into logic programs run by a solver
\citep{jurayj2026logic}, LLM-assisted Prolog formalisation for detecting
inconsistencies in the Internal Revenue Code \citep{yadamsuren2026llm},
contamination-aware comparison of LLM-only and neuro-symbolic tax reasoning
\citep{kordjamshidi2026reasoners}, and structured intermediate
representations for generating decision models from Dutch planning law
\citep{graus2026legal}. These studies evaluate the generated code or the
answers to individual cases. We evaluate a different consequence of an
extraction: the policy conclusions drawn when the extracted law is applied
to a whole population.

\paragraph{Indian legal NLP and tax incidence.}
Pre-trained legal language models for Indian law \citep{paul2023pretrained},
the IL-TUR benchmark \citep{joshi2024iltur} and named-entity recognition in
Indian judgments \citep{kalamkar2022ner} concern case law. We are not aware
of work extracting executable rules from Indian tax statutes. On the
economic side, \citet{datt2022progressivity} measure the progressivity and
redistributive effect of Indian income tax from Income Tax Department data,
\citet{chancel2019indian} combine tax tabulations, surveys and national
accounts into long-run income-distribution series, and
\citet{mukherjee2025gst} estimate the incidence of GST across consumption
fractiles.

\paragraph{Faithful explanations.}
Whether an explanation reflects what a model actually relied on is distinct
from whether it is plausible \citep{jacovi2020faithful}; attention weights
in particular need not provide faithful explanations
\citep{jain2019attention}. ERASER measures faithfulness of extracted
rationales by deleting them or keeping only them
\citep{deyoung2020eraser}; we apply that test to the statute spans our
extractions are grounded in. Shapley values \citep{shapley1953value} are
widely used to attribute a model output to its inputs
\citep{lundberg2017unified}; we use them to attribute a policy divergence to
the provisions that caused it.

\section{From statute to parameters}\label{sec:pipeline}

\subsection{Statute structure}
Indian statutes are typeset with running heads, margin line numbers,
marginal notes and footnotes, and Finance Acts insert quoted text into the
Acts they amend. The parser works on the PDF's text lines with their
positions. It removes running heads (lines repeated on adjacent pages),
margin numbers and footnotes, uses the table of contents as an oracle of the
expected sections, and builds a tree of Part, Chapter, Section,
sub-section, clause, sub-clause, proviso, Explanation, Schedule, Paragraph
and table row. Clause letters and roman numerals that share a form ("(i)")
are told apart by their siblings. Quoted insertions in Finance Acts are
parsed as nested provisions. Every node has exact character offsets into a
canonical text and a content-addressed identifier
(the first 16 hex digits of SHA-256 over statute, path and text), so a node's
identity changes exactly when its text or position does.

\subsection{Rules with typed effects}
A rule is one deontic statement (obligation, prohibition, permission, power,
deeming or definition) with its subject, conditions, action, consequence,
exceptions and cross-references. Each of these is a \emph{span}: offsets into
the canonical text of the provision it was read from. A rule also carries
typed \emph{effects}, the executable content: slab rows (lower and upper bound,
rate, regime, age band, assessment year), rebates, standard deductions,
surcharge bands and caps, cess, and, for other statutes, penalties, fees,
interest and time limits.

\subsection{Extraction systems}
All language models receive the same versioned prompt, one provision per
call, with the enclosing section's opening words as context when a long
provision is split. The systems are GPT-OSS-120B and GPT-OSS-20B
\citep{openai2025gptoss} and Qwen3.8-27B, served by Groq; the local models
Phi-3.5-mini \citep{abdin2024phi3} and Gemma-2-2B \citep{gemmateam2024gemma2}
(4-bit, through Ollama); and a rule-based baseline that reads slab rows and
fee clauses with patterns. Decoding is greedy (temperature 0, fixed seed).
The hosted models were used on free tiers, which fixed two settings.
GPT-OSS-20B runs at low reasoning effort: at the default effort it spent its
whole 4,096-token output budget on reasoning for two of the experiment's
provisions and returned no answer, and the limit of 8,000 tokens per minute
rules out a larger budget. Qwen3.8-27B runs without reasoning: its free tier
allows 1,000 output tokens per minute, which its answers with reasoning
exceeded.

Every quoted span must be found in the provision: exactly, after
normalising whitespace and quotation marks, or by fuzzy alignment with a
similarity of at least 90, in which case the span snaps to the statute's own
text. A field that cannot be grounded is rejected, and every number in an
effect is checked against its quoted span. Each extraction is repeated
$k=4$ times at temperature 0.7; the share of samples that reproduce a rule or
effect is reported as its self-consistency \citep{wang2023selfconsistency}.
All model outputs are cached under a key that covers the provider, model,
generation options, prompt and prompt version, temperature and seed, so a
rerun never calls a model twice and the paper's tables can be regenerated
without network access.

\subsection{One assembler for every system}
The experimental design names, for each reform, which provision determines
which parameter (\cref{tab:design}). The assembler starts from the expert
parameters of the same year and replaces only those targets, using only
effects of the right kind read from the named provision. Slab rows scoped to
the assessment year take precedence over unscoped rows, and only effects
stated for the target's regime (or for both regimes) are used. For a scalar
parameter, effects stated for the target's regime take precedence over those
stated for both; if the remaining candidates disagree the target is
\emph{ambiguous}. A schedule is accepted only if it starts at zero, has no
gap or overlap, ends with an open top band and has non-decreasing rates. Any
target that cannot be filled becomes a review item and the system's run has
\emph{no conclusion}: nothing is filled by default, and a system is never
credited with the expert's value.

\section{Taxpayer population and microsimulation}\label{sec:population}

No unit-level tax or survey data were available to us. The population is
therefore reconstructed from the published Income Tax Return Statistics
(individuals): the number of returns and total gross total income (GTI) in
each income range (table 2.1) are reproduced exactly by weighted points
placed within each range by a maximum-entropy density tilted to the range's
mean, with a Pareto tail in the open top range. Chapter VI-A deductions are
assigned by rank matching the GTI distribution to the returned-income
distribution (table 2.10). The salaried share of each range is the number of
returns reporting salary income in the same range (table 2.2) divided by the
range's returns. Reported GTI is net of the standard deduction of the data
year, which is added back for salaried taxpayers so that each simulated law
applies its own deduction. Populations for later years are aged with nominal
GDP.

Each simulated law is applied to every taxpayer by a calculator that follows
the statutory order: standard deduction, Chapter VI-A deductions (old regime
only), rounding of total income (s.288A), slab tax by age band, rebate with
marginal relief, surcharge with marginal relief and the new regime's cap,
cess, and rounding of tax (s.288B). When both regimes are available a
taxpayer files under the cheaper one. Outcomes are revenue, decile effective
tax rates, and the Kakwani \citep{kakwani1977measurement},
Reynolds--Smolensky \citep{reynolds1977postfisc} and Suits \citep{suits1977measurement} indices.

\section{Measures}\label{sec:measures}

\subsection{Pre-registered conclusions}
For each reform the same population is taxed under the previous year's
expert law and under the reform-year law, either expert-coded or assembled
from a system's rules. Seventeen conclusions were fixed before any system
output was scored: the direction of the revenue change; whether the revenue
change is within 10\% of the expert's; the direction of the change in the
Kakwani index; the decile with the largest fall in effective rate; the
direction of the effective-rate change in each decile; and the regime chosen
by the taxpayer at the 50th, 90th and 99th percentile. For the earliest coded
year the comparison is with a proportional tax raising the same revenue.

Directions are read with materiality thresholds (revenue \rupee500 crore,
effective rate 0.01 percentage points, Kakwani 0.001), and the top gaining
decile is named only when some decile gains by more than the rate threshold.
These thresholds amend the pre-registered definitions: the controlled
error-injection study (\cref{sec:injection}), which uses no system output,
showed that the original near-exact sign tests let a one-rupee shift of a
band boundary flip direction conclusions on reforms with no true change.
Both versions are computed for every run and both are reported.

A system's \emph{flip rate} is the share of conclusions that differ from the
expert's; a run with no conclusion counts every conclusion as flipped.

\subsection{Attribution}
Let $T$ be the set of a reform's targets whose assembled value differs from
the expert's. For a coalition $S \subseteq T$, the hybrid law takes the
system's value for the targets in $S$ and the expert's for the rest, and
$v(S)$ is its divergence from the expert law: the revenue error, the $L_1$
distance between decile effective rates, or the number of flipped
conclusions. The Shapley value \citep{shapley1953value} of each target is
computed exactly over all $2^{|T|}$ coalitions, so the values sum to the
divergence of the full system law. The \emph{minimal restoring set} is the
smallest set of targets whose correction removes every flip.

\subsection{Robustness to population uncertainty}
The population rests on assumptions that the published statistics do not
pin down: the share of taxpayers who choose the cheaper regime (0.25--1),
the scale of Chapter VI-A deductions (0.5--1.5), the Pareto shape of the top
range (1.5--3), the share of taxpayers aged 60--80 (0--0.2) and income growth
beyond the published series (7--12\%). For each run the conclusions of the
expert law and of the system's law are recomputed on 64 scrambled Sobol
draws of these assumptions. A flip's \emph{persistence} is the share of
draws in which it remains; the \emph{noise floor} is the share of draws in
which the expert's own conclusion differs from its conclusion on the
calibrated population. Systems are compared on paired conclusions with
exact McNemar tests \citep{mcnemar1947note}, Holm-corrected
\citep{holm1979simple}.

Two outcomes are possible for a system on a reform: its rules assemble into
a complete law, or a target cannot be filled and the run has no conclusion.
\Cref{tab:headline} and \cref{fig:assembly} show both. Of the \res{reforms} reforms, Qwen3.8-27B's

\res{assembled@gpt-oss-20b}; the rule-based baseline assembled
\res{assembled@rules}, and the two small local models none.

Whenever a system's rules assembled, they reached every one of the
expert's \res{conclusions} conclusions, on every reform and under both the
amended and the original definitions. The extracted laws were not always
identical to the expert's: the largest revenue error of an assembled law
was \rupee\res{maxreverr@gpt-oss-120b}~crore (GPT-OSS-120B and Qwen3.8-27B on
the 2025 reform), caused by a rebate read without its marginal relief
(\cref{tab:attribution}). That error moves revenue by a few per cent of the
reform's cost and changes no conclusion.

The answer to RQ1 is therefore conditional. When the strongest systems
produce a complete law, it supports the same policy conclusions as the
expert's; the risk lies in the cases where they do not, which are examined
next.

\subsection{Which provisions are responsible?}
\input{tables/attribution}
Where an assembled law differed from the expert's, the Shapley attribution
assigns the whole divergence to one provision, the rebate
(\cref{tab:attribution}). The failures of assembly fall into four kinds.

\paragraph{The top of the schedule.} The most frequent error of the strong
systems concerns the open top band: GPT-OSS-120B dropped the highest band of
the substituted table in the Finance (No.~2) Act 2024, and in other
schedules a system closed the top band with an arbitrary upper limit or
omitted it. The assembler rejects a schedule without an open top band.

\paragraph{Regime scoping.} Section 156 of the Income-tax Act 2025 grants
two rebates in one section: \rupee12,500 up to an income of \rupee5 lakh, and
\rupee60,000 up to \rupee12 lakh where income is taxed under s.202(1). All
three strong systems extracted both rebates correctly and attached neither
to its regime; the 2025 Act scopes the second by cross-reference
("chargeable to tax under section 202(1)") rather than by naming the regime.
On the Finance Act 2023 rebate, Qwen3.8-27B split one provision into two
rebate effects, one with the amount and one with the marginal relief.

\paragraph{Missing tables.} In the Finance Act 2025 and 2026 schedules the
two-column layout places each rate on the line after its income band. Some
systems returned no slab rows for some of these items; the rule baseline,
which reads line by line, could read none of them.

\paragraph{Small local models.} Phi-3.5-mini and Gemma-2-2B assembled no
reform. Gemma copied the prompt's template literally (for example
\texttt{"modality": "obligation | prohibition | \ldots"}), so its rules
failed schema validation although the numbers inside them were often right.

\paragraph{A failure that was not the models'.} In one Finance Act 2026
schedule every system failed the same item in the same way. Inspection
showed a parser error: run-in paragraph headings had not been recognised,
and five further paragraphs (rates for co-operative societies, firms and
companies) had been attached to the item. After the parser was corrected
the reform was run again for every system. Since then, any failure shared
by all systems has been checked against the input text before being
attributed to the systems.

\subsection{Are the differences larger than the simulation's uncertainty?}\label{sec:robustness}

\subsection{Which kinds of error matter?}\label{sec:injection}
Thirteen typed errors were injected into the expert law, one target at a time:
a dropped top or middle band, a band boundary moved by one lakh or by one
rupee, a bound read in crore instead of lakh, a rate read from the adjacent
row, the previous year's table, a dropped marginal-relief clause, a dropped
rebate, the previous year's rebate, a misread rebate amount, the rebate of
the other regime, and the previous year's standard deduction.

Of the \res{injections} injections, \res{injectionsflipping} changed at least
one conclusion (\cref{fig:injection}). Three patterns stand out.

First, revenue error and conclusion change are only loosely related. Reading
a band limit in crore instead of lakh moves revenue by up to
\rupee\res{injrevmax@lakh_crore_confusion}~crore yet flips at most
\res{injflipmax@lakh_crore_confusion}\% of a reform's conclusions, while
dropping a rebate's marginal relief moves revenue by at most
\rupee\res{injrevmax@drop_marginal_relief}~crore and flips no conclusion on
the reforms that change the law substantially.

Second, the same error matters most where the law does not change.
On the four restatements of unchanged rate tables and on the re-enactment
of 2025, every true direction is "no change", and any error that moves tax
at all turns it into a change: dropping the marginal relief, harmless on the
2023 and 2025 reforms, flips \res{injflipmax@drop_marginal_relief}\% of the
conclusions on the re-enactment. Tests of
extraction on reforms that change little are therefore stricter, not easier.

Third, the errors the systems actually made are among the most damaging
when they pass through. Taking the other regime's rebate (the s.156
misreading) flips up to \res{injflipmax@other_regime_rebate}\% of the
conclusions and dropping the top band up to
\res{injflipmax@drop_top_band}\%. In the experiment none of these errors
reached a conclusion, because the assembler's structural checks (an open
top band; one rebate per regime) turned them into detected failures. The
validation of executable rules, not only their extraction, decides whether
an extraction error becomes a wrong policy conclusion or a visible gap.

that reads only those spans. For the language models it does not:
GPT-OSS-120B scores \res{faithcomprehensiveness@gpt-oss-120b@dev} against

\section{Threats to validity}\label{sec:threats}

\paragraph{Expert coding.} The parameters were coded by one person with a
citation for every value and checked row by row against the parsed statutes;
the rule baseline reproduces them exactly wherever it can read a table. There
is no independent second human coding.

\paragraph{Population.} No microdata were available. The population
reproduces the published distribution of returns by construction; levels of
tax and reform costs depend on assumptions the statistics do not identify
(\cref{sec:validation}). All comparisons are paired and are recomputed over
the range of those assumptions. Age is not published: the calibrated
population is below 60, so errors in the senior tables appear only in the
robustness draws and in the taxpayer-level check. The salaried share of each
income range is matched from the salary and income tables by range.

\paragraph{Conclusions.} The materiality thresholds amend the pre-registered
definitions; the amendment was motivated by the injection study, which uses
no system output, and both versions are reported. Conclusions within a
reform are correlated, so system rankings rest on the sign tests over
reforms, which have little power with \res{reforms} reforms.

\paragraph{Systems.} All hosted models were used on free tiers, which forced
two documented settings: low reasoning effort for GPT-OSS-20B and no
reasoning for Qwen3.8-27B, whose answers otherwise exceeded the per-minute
output limits. Hosted models change without notice; every output is cached
with the provider's reported model version, and the tables are regenerated
from that cache without network access.

\paragraph{Scope.} Personal income tax of resident individuals at normal
rates; no behavioural response; ten reforms from six Finance Acts and the
Income-tax Act 2025.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

We measured whether rules that language models extract from Indian tax
statutes lead to the same policy conclusions as an expert's coding of the
same provisions. Whenever the strongest systems' rules could be assembled
into a complete law, they did, under every population assumption we tried.
Their errors were concentrated in a few kinds (the top of a rate schedule,
the regime a provision applies to, tables whose layout scatters their
cells), and the controlled study shows that exactly these errors would
change conclusions if they reached the simulation. In our pipeline they did
not, because structural validation of the executable rules turned them into
visible gaps.

For rules-as-code practice this suggests two things. Extraction quality
should be judged by the conclusions the extracted law supports, which surface
metrics do not measure. And the safeguard that matters is validation of the
executable law (complete schedules, scoped benefits, no silent defaults),
which converts errors that would mislead into errors that are seen.

\section{Retrieval over the statute corpus}\label{app:rag}

PRISM's question-answering interface retrieves statute provisions before a
language model answers from them, so retrieval quality bounds the quality of
its answers. We measured it on a benchmark derived from the statutes'
structure. The index holds the \res{ragchunks} extraction units of the
Income-tax Act 2025, the CGST Act, the Code on Wages and six Finance Acts,
each unit's own text with its section heading removed. The queries are
\res{ragqueries} official section headings of the first three Acts (generic
headings that recur are excluded); a query is answered when a unit of the
section it heads is retrieved. The \res{ragunanswerable} headings of the
Digital Personal Data Protection Act, which is not indexed, test whether a
retriever's top score can signal that the corpus has no answer. Headings
are short, written by the drafters and not by users, so the benchmark
measures how well a retriever links a provision's subject to its text;
questions written by the annotators will complement it.

\Cref{tab:rag} shows the results. The dense encoder used by the web
application (MiniLM) retrieves the right section within five results for
\res{ragrfive@minilm} of the queries, fewer than BM25
(\res{ragrfive@bm25}). A stronger encoder (bge-small, \res{ragrfive@bge-small}),
reciprocal-rank fusion with BM25 (\res{ragrfive@hybrid}) and a cross-encoder
re-ranking of the fused candidates (\res{ragrfive@hybrid+rerank}) each
improve on it; the re-ranker also separates unanswerable queries best
(AUROC \res{ragauroc@hybrid+rerank}). Fusing BM25 with the application's
own MiniLM embeddings and re-ranking reaches \res{ragrfive@hybrid-minilm+rerank}
(abstention AUROC \res{ragauroc@hybrid-minilm+rerank}) without re-embedding the
corpus; this is the configuration the application now uses, and its
re-ranker score is the confidence it reports.

\section{A behavioural extension}\label{app:behaviour}

The static microsimulation assumes that taxpayers report what they earn.
To ask the paper's question of a behavioural prediction, we built an
agent-based model of compliance in Mesa \citep{mesa2025} and describe it
in full, following the ODD+D protocol \citep{muller2013describing}, in the
repository (\texttt{docs/ODD+D.md}). Taxpayers sampled from the calibrated
population each period choose how much income to conceal, maximising
expected utility as in \citet{allingham1972income}. A tax-morale cost,
heterogeneous across agents and partly scaled by the honesty of their network
neighbours \citep{myles1996model,hashimzade2013applications}, enters the
choice. Salaried income is reported by employers and is almost impossible to
conceal \citep{kleven2011unwilling}. Beliefs about audit risk adapt to the
audits an agent observes among its neighbours in a small-world network
\citep{watts1998collective}. The law enters twice: the tax schedule, through
the same calculator, and the penalty of s.439 of the Income-tax Act 2025.
That penalty is 50\% of the tax on under-reported income (s.439(9)) and
200\% where the under-reporting results from misreporting (s.439(10)), which
is the rate a deliberate evader faces.

No published Indian data identify the behavioural parameters (perceived
audit risk, risk aversion, tax morale, the evadable share of salaries).
With realistic audit rates the model reproduces the familiar result that
taxpayers without strong morale conceal most of the income they can
\citep{alm2012measuring}: the median predicted gap on non-salaried income is
\res{bhothermedian}. Its levels are therefore not predictions. What it can
show is how much a misread penalty provision matters relative to that
uncertainty.

Across \res{bhdraws} draws of the behavioural parameters, the predicted tax
gap under the expert's penalty lies between \res{bhgapfive} and
\res{bhgapninetyfive} (5th--95th percentile). Applying the under-reporting
rate of 50\% to deliberate concealment raises it by \res{bhdiffmean} on
average (\res{bhdifffive} to \res{bhdiffninetyfive}), roughly a third of
that range. In the Sobol analysis (\res{bhruns} runs) the penalty rate is
the second most influential input (total index \res{bhst@penalty_rate}),
after the evadable share of salaries (\res{bhst@salaried_evadable}) and
ahead of tax morale (\res{bhst@morale}), risk aversion
(\res{bhst@risk_aversion}) and the audit rate (\res{bhst@audit_rate})
(\cref{tab:behaviour_sobol}). Reading the penalty provision correctly
therefore matters for behavioural predictions about as much as the
behavioural assumptions themselves.

\Cref{tab:behaviour_systems} shows what the systems read from s.439(10).
\res{bhcorrect} of them extract the 200\% rate exactly, and so give the
expert's predictions. The others extract no rate and leave the model
without a penalty, mirroring the failures of assembly in the main
experiment. Seed-to-seed variation of the tax gap falls from
\res{bhsdsmall} with 1,000 agents to \res{bhsdlarge} with 10,000; the
paired comparisons use common random numbers.
